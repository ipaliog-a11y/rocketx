# Untitled — Research-First Launch Company

A game concept: you are the chief engineer of a private launch startup. You do **not** fly the rockets. You design them, pay for tests, bid contracts, and watch the vehicle try to do the job. Research is the main loop. The company is the pressure: cash, calendar, reputation, and the chance the firm dies before first orbit.

This repo holds early design decisions only. Nothing here is a build spec.

## Pitch

You don’t fly the rocket. You argue with physics until a contract can fly it, and you watch the argument happen.

- Design vehicles from engineering domains (aerodynamics, flight control, engines, propellant chemistry, and later structures, thermal, avionics, manufacturing, ground systems).
- Send them to work on contracts.
- Watch takeoff, ascent, staging, and insertion as a spectator with a readable debrief.
- Learn real rocket ideas because you needed them, not because a tooltip said so.
- Choices lock you into a technical identity. Two competent players should finish as different companies.
- A small roguelike wrap: one company is one run. The firm can die. Your understanding persists.

## Pillars

1. **Research in the foreground.** Experiments, test stands, and failure modes are the gameplay.
2. **Company in the background.** Cash, schedule, facilities, and customers exist to make engineering painful, not to be a tycoon spreadsheet.
3. **No piloting.** Design → test → dispatch → watch → autopsy.
4. **Options matter.** Domains are competing answers to the same problem, not four parallel XP bars.
5. **No perfect vehicle.** Certainty is bought with test flights. 100% failure-free does not exist.
6. **Early fail is allowed.** Spend everything and miss LEO, the run ends.
7. **Ambition is a bet.** Hard contracts introduce regimes you have never tested.
8. **Small roguelike.** Physics stay honest. The *situation* is seeded. Knowledge carries between runs; the company does not.

## Core loop

**Bid → decide what you refuse to know → pay for the tests you do run → watch one flight → update the model → LEO-and-alive or broke.**

After first orbit the same loop scales: new regime, new ignorance, new chance to overreach.

## What this is not

- Not Kerbal. No manual gravity turns, docking, or building in a parts hangar as the product.
- Not EarthX / Space Company Simulator. Launch cadence and department trees are not the toy.
- Not a linear “unlock Falcon → unlock Starship” campaign.
- Not a full roguelike smash-another-seed-in-20-minutes game. A run should last long enough to learn something.

## Status

Text prototype complete. All six steps of DESIGN.md §15 exist: one pad, one stand and one cash pile; two domains (**engines + aerodynamics**); suborbital and small LEO contracts; tests that reveal named failure modes; an ascent with a debrief; and a run that can die. It plays as a script of steps from the command line. No name locked.

Built so far: a two-dimensional ascent sim in the plane of the trajectory, flown against a mission. It goes from the pad to orbit insertion, or to apogee if the vehicle falls short.

- US Standard Atmosphere 1976 up to 86 km, vacuum above 150 km
- Drag from a Mach-dependent Cd curve with a transonic rise. Frontal area comes from the widest stage or the fairing
- Engine thrust falls with ambient pressure (F = F_vac − p·A_e). Nozzle flow separation is flagged by the Summerfield criterion
- Earth rotation: the share of the spin that lies in the trajectory plane moves the pad and the air. Due east from 28.5° N starts the vehicle at 408 m/s; a retrograde sun-synchronous track starts it 60 m/s backwards
- Missions: a launch site plus an optional insertion target (periapsis, apoapsis, inclination, tolerances). The launch azimuth comes from the target inclination. A pad can't reach an inclination below its own latitude
- Three pitch programmes: vertical; a gravity turn (pitch kick, then prograde) with an optional upper-stage pitch ramp; or a time-based pitch schedule
- Guidance cuts the last stage off once the orbit has the target's energy. The insertion is scored in or out of spec, and leftover propellant is the margin
- Staging, max-q, Mach 1, load-budget, side-load (q·α) and acceleration-limit events
- Δv bookkeeping relative to the ground: delivered Δv split into gravity, drag, back-pressure and steering losses
- A text debrief that names the physics, including why an insertion missed
- Engine test stand. Each engine has hidden truths rolled from the run's seed: real Isp, hard start, combustion instability onset and chamber life. Hot-fires on a sea-level stand or an altitude cell reveal only what they reach, cost cash and calendar days, and destroy the test engine when something fails. A vacuum bell can't run against sea-level air, so the sea-level stand gives it only a 2 s start check: a cheap way to catch a hard start before booking the altitude cell. Fixes remove a mode the stand has seen, cost some performance, and make earlier proof void
- Wind tunnels. Each outer shape has hidden aero truths: real drag against the hand-estimated curve, the real transonic drag rise, and buffet on the fairing shoulder (likelier on a hammerhead fairing). CFD is cheap and gets drag about half right, but can't see buffet. The subsonic tunnel measures drag. The transonic tunnel measures the drag rise and shows buffet. Vortex generators fix buffet for about 3% drag. Buffet that drives the load past the structural budget breaks the vehicle up near Mach 1
- Before flight: a list of what the vehicle is flying on trust, and a forecast that flies the vehicle as measured, including any failure seen and not fixed. In flight, engines perform and fail as built, and the debrief points at the firing that would have caught the failure

The trajectory stays in one plane. Plane changes, the out-of-plane part of the pad's motion and coast-and-relight insertions aren't modelled.

- Pitch programme re-tuning. The trajectory office searches the gravity turn's kick angle and upper-stage ramp against the company's model of the vehicle: measured Isp and drag where tests measured them, the drawings where not, and known buffet. Inside half the tolerance it prefers propellant margin. Engine failures are left out, since no pitch steers around them. A re-tune is adopted only if its prediction reaches orbit

A re-tune is only as good as the testing behind it. Tuned on the drawings and flown on real engines, Meridian misses. With accurate test data and engine failures set aside, re-tuning took it from 0 to 13 of 25 seeds in spec (measured before engines were built from parts). Of the rest, 3 buffet whatever the pitch, and 9 lack the Δv for a 200 kg payload once real Isp is known.

- Company and contracts. Cash, a calendar, and a reputation record tagged by what happened. A failed test flight goes on the record but costs no reputation, and customers don't hold it against the company: nobody's payload was lost. A seeded board offers suborbital crumbs, LEO work, an SSO contract that punishes a greedy bid, and an equatorial customer the pad can't serve. Contracts have payouts, penalties, deadlines, a reputation bar, and customers who refuse certain records
- The bid screen shows what a contract pays and risks, the build cost, regimes the design has never flown, everything on trust, and a forecast with the contract's payload
- Vehicles cost cash and days to build. A launch uses one up. In-spec delivery pays in full, an out-of-spec orbit pays half, and a loss or a missed deadline costs the penalty. Flights are tests too: telemetry teaches what the flight reached, and a stage that burns to empty tanks without a failure proves its engine for the whole burn, stand firing or not
- The run ends when the cash goes negative. The summary breaks down where the money went and what the record says
- Launch licences. A new company holds only the suborbital licence. The orbital one needs two flights above 100 km without losing the vehicle, a reputation of 0 or better, $250k and a 20-day review. Orbital contracts and orbital test flights are refused without it. The crewed-flight tier comes later (see crew, below)
- A generated board on top of the hand-tested contracts: about 80 contracts per seed over three years, across sounding science, microgravity, technology demos, rideshares, smallsats, weather, constellations, space agencies and crew. Payloads and orbits stay inside what the starting launchers reach with typical engines, with a margin. Recurring series (a university's aurora campaign, three constellations, the rideshare broker) post an instance every month or two. Deliver one and the rest pay 5% more; fail or miss one and the customer withdraws the rest. A series can be committed to in one go, signing every remaining launch (even ones not yet posted) onto the calendar. Delivering every launch of a series, however each was signed, pays a 15% bonus on top and +1 reputation
- Engines built from parts. An engine is a chamber size (9, 25, 35 or 50 kN) plus four parts: a feed cycle (pressure-fed, electric pump, gas generator), an injector (showerhead, pintle, coaxial swirl), chamber cooling (ablative liner, film, regenerative) and a nozzle (sea-level, altitude, vacuum, large vacuum). Each option publishes ranges, and each hidden quirk belongs to the part that causes it: hard start to the cycle, instability to the injector, chamber life to the cooling, flow separation to the nozzle, and the Isp shortfall to injector and nozzle. A part is one option at one size, so sister engines share parts: the two Harriers share cycle, injector and cooling
- What tests learn lands on the parts. Firing Harrier-SL proves its injector for Harrier-Vac too; a burn-through at 60% of one engine's rated life predicts 60% of its sister's. A fix is a new revision of the part that owns the mode, paid once and applied to every engine built with it, and only that part's proof has to be redone
- Engine development: put parts together into a new engine. Parts already in production are free to reuse and come with what tests know; new parts cost development money and time and start unproven. Refining an injector or nozzle (R&D against measured Isp) closes part of the gap to the top of its range, up to three times, and the gain has to be measured again. A design's stage can be re-engined while none of it is built
- The drawing board: design a vehicle from one or two stages (diameter, tank length, engine and engine count) and a fairing. Propellant follows from tank volume and dry mass from a simple structures model (engine mass by thrust, tank structure by propellant, heavier for pressure-fed tanks, plus avionics, interstage and fairing). Redrawn presets come out within about 10% of the hand-drawn ones. The board refuses what can't work (a booster bell that separates on the pad, too many engines for the diameter, a stack too slender, liftoff thrust below weight) and warns about what is risky (low thrust to weight, a hammerhead fairing). Drawing up costs the office's time, plus tooling the first time a tank or fairing diameter is used
- A sizing guide: say what the rocket is for (a contract, an orbit or an apogee, and a payload) and it searches stage sizes and the company's engines with the rocket equation against what the job takes, then offers the cheapest, a balanced and a most-margin design. Orbital picks re-tune into spec on typical engines; sounding picks are flown to confirm the apogee
- Production lines. A build is an order: paid up front, built on a line while the company keeps testing and flying, and rolled out on its ready day. Orders queue on the line that frees first; a launch with the vehicle still on the line waits for rollout. A second and third line ($2.5M and $4M) build in parallel. The factory learns: each doubling of a design's build count cuts its build time to 85%, down to 55% of the first
- High-energy missions. Orbits above 500 km go to a 200 km parking orbit, coast, and relight the upper stage: once to raise the high point (geostationary transfer, trans-lunar injection) or to escape with a given C3, and once more at the high point to circularise. Each burn steers prograde and cuts off on the energy of the orbit it aims for. Coasts are integrated with a longer step and replayed from above the orbit
- Relights are a regime of their own. A stage that has already run must restart in vacuum, and a relight failure, owned by the feed cycle, strands the payload in its parking orbit. The altitude cell runs restart tests; a restart kit fixes the mode for every engine sharing the cycle
- Bigger hardware: 3, 100 and 250 kN chambers, a storable (hypergolic) cycle that lights reliably for kick stages and deep space, 2.4 and 3.0 m tanks, and three-stage designs. The guide sizes the burns after the parking orbit from vis-viva, adds a kick stage beyond about 11 km/s, and asks for 8% margin when a mission relights
- High-energy work on the board from about day 340 (navigation) and 380 (geostationary): geostationary comsats, navigation satellites at 1,200 km, lunar probes, deep-space probes and the Skyweb mega-constellation (a 500–750 kg batch every month). Payloads are sized against a developed 100 kN gas-generator booster with a margin; the tests fly the heaviest of each
- Engines are priced from their parts, so a pressure-fed engine costs about half as much per kN as a gas-generator one
- Work on tracks that run side by side: the sea-level stand, the altitude cell, the engineering office (fixes, refinements, engine development, drawings, re-tunes), the wind tunnel and the regulator. Each job is paid when booked, starts when its track is free, and shows what it found only on the day it finishes. Jobs on the same hardware keep the order they were booked in, so a re-test waits for the fix to its parts. A lost test engine ties up its stand while a new one is built. A second sea-level stand ($1.5M), a second altitude cell ($5M) and more engineering teams ($1M, $2M) add capacity. The command line and the bots still run each job to its end before the next
- Heavy lift: a 600 kN gas-generator chamber (its development, fixes and test engines cost the most of any size) and 4.0 m tanks ($1.2M and 45 days of tooling). The sizing guide reaches past 16 m of core and 6 m of upper stage on the 3 m class and up, so it can size a 2.9 t station payload: eight or nine 600 kN engines on a 4 m core, about $61M a rocket
- Crew. A capsule ($8M, 150 days in the engineering office; needs the orbital licence) for two crew, 2.4 t, under a 500 kg abort tower dropped at T+150 s. Its abort system has hidden flaws like an engine's: a tower that misfires, parachutes that fail, a capsule that breaks up pulling away in thick air. A pad abort on the new test range ($1.5M, 20 days) shows the first two; an in-flight abort spends a built rocket and fires at max-q, and only counts if it really came above 15 kPa. Each flaw has a fix in the office, and a fix means flying both tests again. A rocket is crew-rated once its engines are proven for their full burns, its last three flights reached orbit cleanly and both abort tests have passed. The crewed-flight licence wants five orbital deliveries, reputation 5, both abort tests and a crew-rated rocket ($2M, 90 days). Station resupply (the capsule with cargo, $75–90M a flight, from about day 500) needs only the orbital licence and builds the record; crew rotations ($130M) come later. If a rocket fails under a crew, the abort fires and the hidden truth decides: crew safe and an ordinary failed contract, or crew lost, the crewed-flight licence revoked, $50M and −10 reputation. The company survives
- An engine identical to one the company already has (same parts, same size) can't be developed again
- Market eras (`src/sim/market.ts`). As reputation grows, bigger customers come to the company. Era 2 opens the first time reputation reaches 4 and era 3 at 8; an era never closes. On the day one opens, light one-off work to low orbit not yet posted (smallsats, rideshares, weather and agency satellites up to 250 kg, outside a series) thins to 40%, then 15%, by a seeded roll per contract, and the era's own work is posted from then on. Era 2 wants 375–450 kg buses at 28.5°, just past what Meridian lifts and within what a rocket drawn with the starting Harrier engines can carry (about $8–9M, paying about 1.6 times that), and 300–500 kg SSO imagers. Era 3 wants 1–2.5 t buses and 0.8–1.5 t SSO platforms. The imagers and everything in era 3 need a developed 100 or 250 kN engine. Era work has its own random stream, so the rest of the board is the same with or without eras. Signed, series and already-posted work is never touched, and an old save moves up on its next tick. The Contracts header shows the era and what opens the next, Home › Company shows it under reputation, and a banner marks the day it changes
- The company's own network (`src/sim/network.ts`). From market era 2, with the orbital licence, the engineering office can charter a constellation ($6M, 90 days): 24 slots at 400 km, 51.6°, a direct ascent any orbital design can reach. Satellites come from a supplier ($250k each, 20 kg, delivered in 60 days) and go up on the company's own rockets in a 10 kg dispenser: a Meridian carries about five, an era-2 rocket about twelve. Launch › Network shows how many each design carries on the sizing guide's paper estimate, and a re-tune and the forecast settle the real flight. In spec, each satellite takes the lowest empty slot; out of spec it spends half its life reaching it; a failure loses the batch. Service starts with 8 working and pays $3M a month at 24, in proportion between; ground operations cost $50k a month from the charter on. Each satellite has a hidden life of five years, give or take 30%, and one in twenty fails in its first three months, so a network needs replacement launches. Launch › Control is its control center: every satellite in its slot round the orbit coloured by health with the coverage gaps marked, coverage, income and mean age, the last twelve bills, a replacement plan that orders what is short or near its design life, an operations log, and a health table from telemetry (life used, station-keeping fuel, battery). The real end of a satellite's life only shows when it comes
- The starting set (`src/starters.ts`): a company begins with two drawing-board designs, the sizing guide's cheapest pick for the work the board posts, so they fly the same physics as anything the player draws. Pathfinder is one pressure-fed Kestrel-SL on a 0.6 m finned core ($0.73M, 80 kg to about 156 km on paper, where the board asks at most 103 km at that mass). Meridian is eleven Harrier-SL on a 1.2 m core under a 1.0 m Harrier-Vac upper stage, guided ($4.98M); with a re-tune it reaches every orbit on the board's capacity table, and it ships tuned for 200 kg to 300 km at 28.5°. Both have shells with hidden knock-downs and Meridian has a hidden attitude-loop truth, rolled when the company is founded. The hand-written presets they replace (no shells, no control loop, cheaper than anything the board could draw) stay in `src/vehicles.ts` as command-line teaching cases and test fixtures
- Engine work scales with the chamber. Developing a part, fixing it, the propellant a firing burns and the test engine a failure destroys all cost less on a 9 kN chamber than on a 250 kN one (a 35 kN chamber is the reference). A destroyed test engine costs 60% of its build price (pumps and valves are salvaged) and 12–18 days on its stand. At 35 kN a fix is $90–220k and 12–25 days: about half what it was. Developing a new engine with one new part takes about two to three weeks
- Achievements worked out from the record: first firing, first flight above 100 km, first orbit, the orbital licence, a fleet of five, $10M earned, a completed series and so on
- Reusable boosters. A two-stage design on the drawing board can bring its booster back, to a drone ship downrange or to the pad, with landing legs (+6% of the booster's dry mass), grid fins and a base heat shield. The booster cuts off with a reserve of its propellant still in the tanks; the upper stage flies on without it. After separation the booster flies its own path home on the same Earth, gravity and air. It flips, then (to the pad) burns back until its predicted landing, drag included, is the pad. It burns again on the way down until it's slow enough to meet the air at the shield's limit: the Allen–Eggers peak-heating estimate from its mass and flight-path angle. Grid fins steer it toward the pad, and a last burn lands it, leaning to close the gap. Every burn after the first is a relight, so an engine whose relight truth fails can't come home. It is lost if it runs dry, burns up, lands harder than 6 m/s or misses the pad or the heaving deck. A drone ship landing needs about 12% of the booster's propellant, the pad about 20%. On a small rocket that much reserve stages it so low and slow that the upper stage can't hold attitude in the air. The board plots the paper landing margin against the reserve, with both redlines
- A booster that lands keeps its serial (B1001…), its flights and wear nobody sees. A hot entry or a hard touchdown wears it faster. The new booster hangar turns it around (12% of its build cost, 20 days) or overhauls it (30%, 45 days, takes out a third of the wear and measures what's left). Then a new upper stage is stacked on it for the upper stage's cost plus $100k of integration. Every booster has a hidden life of about ten flights, ±35%; past three-quarters of it, its engines can fail on the way up. Crew and series customers won't fly on a booster that has flown until the company has three clean reflights

## Balance

`npm run playtest` plays whole company runs with bots across seeds. The bots decide only from what a player sees: test reports, forecasts and the board. They fly sounding work (or test flights) until they can apply for the orbital licence, then take orbital work. There are four strategies: careful (full-duration firings with fixes, a transonic tunnel run, a proof test of the shells, the GNC lab's three tests, re-tuning, and bids only on a good forecast), cheap (5 s startup firings and CFD only), reckless (no tests) and greedy (careful, plus the SSO bid regardless of forecast). All of them start from the drawn Pathfinder and Meridian. Current results over 30 seeds and a two-year horizon:

| Strategy | Survive | Reach orbit | Median cash at end |
|---|---|---|---|
| careful | 30 | 30 | $33.4M |
| greedy | 6 | 21 | −$0.9M |
| cheap | 16 | 3 | $6.5M |
| reckless | 14* | 2 | $5.2M |

\* Some reckless bots never earn the orbital licence; they stop trying and sit on their cash, so they count as alive.

Since engines share parts, testing one Harrier teaches about the other, so careful play reaches orbit sooner (median day 322, down from 406 before parts) and richer. Failures are shared too: a flaw in the shared cycle, injector or liner sinks both stages, and the greedy SSO bid kills 24 of 30. Student payloads and recovery flights give a company with a bad record a way back, so fewer runs stall; reckless bots now fly more and fail more. The bots build and launch straight away, so they don't use parallel builds or the drawing board, and they leave high-energy work (anything that needs a parking orbit and relights) alone: they only fly the starting Meridian. The table is unchanged by the high-energy update. Since the factory learns and failed test flights stopped costing reputation, careful play ends about $10M richer and cheap and reckless bots survive a little more often (from 34.9M, 15 and 10). The crew update leaves the bots alone: they never develop a capsule. Cheaper engine work (development, fixes and lost test engines scaled by chamber size) moves careful play to 30 of 30 with $46.2M and lets two more greedy runs survive; the bots never develop engines, so most of the change is in fixes and test-engine losses. Testing pays, and testing the wrong question costs nearly as much as not testing. Before the generated board, careful survived 24 of 30 with $15.1M: with more work posted, careful play is now comfortable and the money has nothing to buy. That waits on things worth spending on (a second pad and production line, bigger vehicles) rather than lower payouts. Payroll is $5k a day. An altitude-cell firing costs $120k plus $1.5k a second on a 35 kN engine (less on a smaller one), so a full-duration upper-stage firing is about a tenth of a Meridian.

**Designing your own rocket.** Two more bots, `designer` and `designer-fast`, play the same way but draw their own orbital rocket with the sizing guide instead of flying the starting Meridian. `designer` also proof-tests the shells and runs the modal survey, IMU calibration and hardware-in-the-loop; `designer-fast` only fires the engines and runs the tunnel. Over 6 seeds:

| Strategy | Survive | Reach orbit | Median first orbit | Median cash at end | Deliveries | Vehicles lost |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| careful (starting Meridian) | 6 | 6 | day 339 | $37.3M | 50 | 2 (buckled) |
| designer | 6 | 6 | day 349 | $32.9M | 50 | 1 (buckled) |
| designer-fast | 6 | 6 | day 302 | $35.6M | 55 | 2 (buckled) |
| designer-reuse | 6 | 6 | day 391 | $32.1M | 53 | 0 |

**The drawn starting set.** The starting Pathfinder and Meridian used to be hand-written presets with no shells and no control loop: they could never buckle or lose attitude in wind, and the old Meridian ($4.0M) undercut the cheapest rocket the guide could draw for its own job ($4.98M). With them, careful play ended with $46.2M against $26.2M for a company that drew its own rocket, and the drawing board never paid. The drawn set flies the full physics: Meridian costs about $1M more a flight and wants a $445k proof and GNC campaign. To keep the economy where it was, generated rideshare, smallsat and constellation work and the opening rideshare broker pay $1M more, and companies start with $11–16M instead of $9–14M. Careful play ends about $10M poorer than with the old presets, but with 30 of 30 alive and all 30 in orbit; drawing your own rocket now ends level with flying the starter. Cheap and reckless companies survive about as often as before (16 and 14 of 30), but fewer reach orbit (3 and 2, from 8 and 3): most that die are finished before first orbit, unable to afford another try with a $5M rocket. Two buckled Meridians in 30 careful runs are the cost of shells nobody can see: the proof test shows the knock-down, and a margin chosen on paper can still be short of it. Three bugs turned up on the way. A flight's payout landed after the payroll for its days on the pad, so a company a few thousand short for one afternoon was declared bankrupt with $8.8M arriving the same day; the books are now checked once the customer has paid. The bots signed a contract, then spent a re-tune's three days and missed the deadline; they now check it again. And a new company never rolled the hidden truths of a starting design's shells and control loop, which a drawn design gets when it is drawn.

Drawing a new rocket costs about four months (drawing office, new tooling, proving its engines and shape) and the proof and GNC tests about $445k, so a company that only needs Meridian's job is better off flying Meridian; the drawing board pays for jobs the starting set can't do. Skipping the proof and GNC tests saves money and a month, and costs rockets: two buckled in six runs. These tables include market eras; within two years they cost the designer bots a few million against careful, which reaches era 2 with a rocket it already flies. Getting here took three fixes the bots found: the guide's shortlist dropped the cheap stacks that still cleared its margin bar after redrawing (it suggested $7M rockets where a $4.9M one would do); its shells weren't sized for wind (the guide now flies six days of weather and pressurises the tanks for the worst side load at the weakest knock-down, plus a quarter); and the first orbital flight could miss because a fixed steering programme is very sensitive to the one drag figure nobody can measure before flying. Guided vehicles' last stages now steer closed-loop to the target orbit, as a flight computer does; the old hand-written presets keep their open programmes.

**Market eras.** The bots now see eras too, and a testing bot moves up: when era work is posted that no rocket in its fleet can deliver on its forecast, it draws the guide's balanced pick for it (if it holds the rocket's cost plus $4M), books the drawing and the new rocket's tests on the tracks, keeps flying meanwhile, and flies each job on the cheapest rocket whose forecast delivers. `--no-move` turns that off and `--horizon` sets the run length in days. Careful play over 30 seeds:

| Careful | Median cash | Deliveries | Era work delivered |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 years, moves up | $33.4M | 260 | 9 |
| 2 years, stays on Meridian | $35.7M | 278 | — |
| 3 years, moves up | $56.4M | 400 | 84 |
| 3 years, stays on Meridian | $58.9M | 471 | — |

Era 2 opens around day 465 and era 3 around day 700, so within two years eras barely matter: a bot flies about ten jobs, and even the thinned board has more than that. Over three years the thinning bites: a company that stays on Meridian ends $9.5M poorer than it did when only smallsats and rideshares thinned ($68.4M). Moving up gets within $2.5M of staying put. The gap is the bots' one-job-at-a-time flying (the heavier rocket takes longer to build, and every job waits for the last); a company running lines in parallel takes the better margin on each era flight (about $5M on a bus against $2.8M on a Meridian smallsat) without giving up the others. Most era work stays out of the bots' reach: 302 jobs needed a 100 or 250 kN engine, and the bots never develop engines. Getting here took three fixes. The bot first judged its fleet by each design's drawn payload, so it never tried Meridian (drawn for 200 kg, good for about 340) on a 300 kg bus and drew rockets it didn't need; era 2's buses now start at 375 kg, just past Meridian. Drawing and testing blocked the bot for about 100 days; they are now booked on the tracks. And it re-tuned every rocket for every era job it looked at, three days each, until it checked by forecast alone.

**Reusable boosters.** The sizing guide can size a reusable rocket: choose a drone ship or the pad and it fits the stack and its landing reserve together. The pick must land with 100 m/s to spare on paper and keep the usual 4% Δv margin, on typical engines. Each pick shows what a reflight costs (a new upper stage plus a turnaround) and the flight on which it pays back against expending. For a 150 kg LEO job:

| | Expendable | Reusable |
|---|---|---|
| Build cost | $4.50M | $7.94M |
| Landing reserve | — | 12% |
| Reflight cost | — | $2.13M |
| Pays back | — | on the 3rd flight, if every booster lands |

Back to the pad, nothing that size flies: the reserve stages the booster at 11 kPa and the upper stage can't hold attitude. The guide says so.

`designer-reuse` tests like `designer` and flies the guide's reusable pick once it holds the rocket's cost plus $6M: from the start if it can, otherwise after first orbit with at least a year left. It keeps the expendable rocket for jobs the reusable one can't carry. It turns landed boosters around, overhauls them every fifth flight, retires them when worn, and stacks new upper stages on them.

With the larger starting cash the reuse bot can now afford its reusable rocket: 19 of 22 boosters landed (three missed the pad) and 11 flights went up on a flown booster, at $4.04M a flight in building and the hangar against $4.13M for `designer`. Over two years it ends level with `designer`. Before the drawn starting set, with $10–14M in hand when they drew their orbital rocket, none of the six could afford the reusable one at the start. An earlier, bolder version sized against ideal engines and drew a $6.5M reusable rocket. Two companies that could afford it from the start ended with $41M against $30M and $19M for `designer`, and two that couldn't afford it went under. Reuse pays for a company that can afford it early; the guide's careful sizing makes it a mid-game investment. Getting here took three fixes the bot found:
- The pitch re-tune lofted reusable ascents. The booster fell back almost vertically at 1.75 km/s and burned up. The re-tune now counts a lost booster against a steering.
- The entry burn lit three engines whatever the booster. It now lights a third of them.
- The bot sized its reserve against a razor-thin ascent margin, so the paper ascent reached orbit and the real one fell short.

Not modelled yet: loans or investors, and the rest of DESIGN.md's later domains (thermal, manufacturing, ground systems).

The stack is drawn to scale from the same layout the physics uses: the nose by its shape and fineness, tanks, interstages, fins and engine bells, with the centre of mass ⊕ and centre of pressure ◇ at liftoff and the static margin between them in calibres. Each part has a PARAMETER choice and a curve slider: stage diameter and tank length (Δv and T/W), ullage pressure and safety factor (q·α capacity and dry mass), nose fineness and fairing diameter (Cd at Mach 1.2 and fairing mass), jettison altitude (heating, with ▲ MIN 100 KM and ▲ 1135 W/m²), fin span, chord and sweep (margin at liftoff and max-q, ▲ NEUTRAL). Discrete choices for nose shape, tank material (with its one-off qualification cost), engine, engine count, fin count and section, and adding or removing stages and fins. Seven readouts compare against the base revision; DIFF overlays it dashed. DRAW UP makes the next revision (Pathfinder → Pathfinder B) through the usual go-ahead, and is blocked with the reason while any limit is hit. PROOF TEST, TO TUNNEL and SIMULATE go straight to the stand, the tunnel and the simulator.
- **The vehicle model** (`src/sim/geometry.ts`, `aerodynamics.ts`, `stability.ts`, `structures.ts`): every design drawn on the new screen or suggested by the guide carries a geometry, and so do the starting Pathfinder and Meridian; the old hand-written presets (now command-line teaching cases) and designs saved before the change keep their old figures and fly exactly as before. Diameters are continuous (0.6–4 m, tooling reused within 3%). Drag is built up per component after OpenRocket's method: skin friction by Reynolds number with compressibility, nose pressure drag by shape and fineness, stagnation drag on every forward-facing step (a hammerhead pays for its shoulder), base drag less the engines' exit area, and fin drag by section. Stability is Barrowman's: nose, shoulders, body lift and fins (subsonic and supersonic, with body interference) give the centre of pressure against the draining mass stack; an unguided rocket needs a calibre of margin at liftoff and half a calibre at max-q. Tank walls are sized by hoop stress from ullage and the hydrostatic head at the design load, with a minimum gauge per material; bending capacity is the knocked-down buckling strength of a pressure-stabilised cylinder (NASA SP-8007), which sets q·α and max-q. The real knock-down is a hidden roll per material and diameter: a proof test on the stand reveals it, and a shell weaker than its drawing buckles in flight. The fairing comes off at its jettison altitude; below 100 km is a hard limit and the debrief notes when the payload saw more than 1135 W/m². Each shape also hides a supersonic drag factor no tunnel here reaches: CFD gives half of it and a flight past Mach 1.5 measures it.

Contracts and signed flights lead with a small drawing of their orbit (straight up, low orbit, polar, transfer ellipse, escape, constellation, station). - **Explained once, then out of the way:** what each page and tab is for sits behind an ⓘ beside its title, and long notes (re-tuning, test flights, the Δv a job needs, crew rating, the steering) sit behind an ⓘ beside what they explain. The Help button in the top bar has a getting-started guide and a searchable glossary of 28 terms. - **Rockets → Simulator:** fly any design on paper, free and with no days passing, for its test flight or a signed contract, at any payload, with its current steering or the best steering a re-tune would find. It flies what tests have measured and the drawings for the rest; nothing fails, and failures tests have already seen are listed against the flight. Where Isp or drag was never measured it also flies the worst and best cases from the catalogue ranges (CFD halves the drag range, a tunnel run or a firing closes it), drawn as red and green traces behind the replay. It estimates the spare payload from the propellant left, and never reads the hidden truth. "Simulate" on a design's drawing and on the Launch page opens it.
- **Launch → Crew:** the capsule, the abort system with its tests, flaws and fixes, an in-flight abort on any built rocket strong enough to lift the capsule, crew-rated status per design and what the crewed-flight licence still wants.
- **Testing:** engine firings, an engine Design screen (below), and a Parts tab of short cards: each shows the part's maturity, the one figure its slot is judged on (start and relight, stable seconds, chamber life, vacuum measurement, Isp) and what it needs next, and opens on tap into its record, revisions and test plan. Filter chips show one slot, or only the parts that need work. Each part card names what it still has to prove, and "Test it" opens the Firing tab on the cheapest engine built with it, with that firing set up. Injector and nozzle cards show the Isp their engines deliver against the drawings, the range the part type allows, what the last refinement bought once re-measured, and what another is worth in Δv and payload on each design.
- **Testing → Design (engines):** the first screen in the design-office look of `UI-DESIGN.md` (dark, cyan data, one amber accent, red only for limits, IBM Plex Mono numbers, 1px lines, square corners, an 8px grid). A part tree with a go/no-go readiness list, the engine as a schematic drawn to scale from the model (the bell from ε and the throat, the feed system as a P&ID that changes with the cycle), a parameter strip, seven readouts and a title block with a rubber stamp. Each part has a curve slider: the marker rides the real performance curve, with redlines marked ▲ and a word (SEP @ SL, PUMP LIMIT, MIN MARGIN, BLADE REDLINE, TURBINE MAX), plus a range input for arrow keys and a number input for typed values. Parts and schematic parts are buttons (Tab, Enter or Space). Commit makes the next revision as a development job (Harrier-SL → Harrier-SL B) through the usual go-ahead; a released revision goes straight to the stand. Engines drawn before the model keep their own figures until a parameter moves.
- **The engine model** (`src/sim/enginePhysics.ts`): chamber pressure, expansion ratio, turbine inlet temperature and pump discharge (tank pressure when pressure-fed) are continuous. An ideal-nozzle thrust coefficient (γ 1.2) and c* give thrust and Isp through the chamber's fixed throat; a gas generator pays Isp for the turbine flow its pumps need; mass grows with wall pressure, pump power and bell size; rated life falls with chamber pressure; a soft feed (pump margin under 1.3) raises the injector's instability chance through one extra seeded roll. Each cycle's reference point reproduces the old catalogue figures exactly; the part choices stay technologies with their own hidden quirks, and the nozzle part is the band of ε the bell falls in. Limits: feed margin 1.2, blade redline 950 K, 6% turbine flow, motor power, 35 bar tanks; separation at sea level is a warning (upper stage only), as the builder already treats it. Sea-level performance of new engines is better than the old exit-area formula gave: a 100 kN gas generator's sea-level Isp is 273 s on the model against 242 s before, closer to real engines.
- **Guidance and control** (`src/sim/control.ts`, `wind.ts`, `gncTests.ts`, `dispersions.ts`): a guided design drawn on the new screen flies an attitude loop. Each step finds the gimbal angle that balances the air's moment about the centre of mass (normal force at the centre of pressure, from the angle of attack with the gusts in it), followed only as fast as the loop's bandwidth; past the gimbal range for half a second, the vehicle is lost. The loop's own health is one oscillation that a healthy loop damps and that grows when the airframe diverges faster than half the loop's crossover, when the first bending mode gets into the loop (its gain falls with the square of the frequency gap, and a notch filter scheduled with the mode takes out nine tenths of it when it sits within 10% of the real mode), when propellant slosh (its first mode rising with acceleration) sits near the loop in tanks without baffles, when the actuators run at their rate limit, or in the Mach band of a flight-software gain error. Past 5°, loss of control; a bending-driven one is control-structure coupling. The IMU drifts the attitude the vehicle thinks it holds, which shows up in insertion accuracy. Wind is seeded pink noise with a jet stream: the day's balloon takes out nine tenths of the mean, the gusts load the airframe (q·α, so shells now meet them too). Each guided design hides its real bending frequency, slosh gains, IMU drift and, one time in five, a software error. The attitude loop runs in thick air (above 5 kPa); wind loads the airframe but doesn't push the trajectory. A guided vehicle eases an upper stage onto its programme over 15 s instead of snapping to it, and its last stage steers closed-loop to the target orbit (the thrust angle that brings it to the target altitude with no vertical speed left at burnout, re-solved every step), so a booster that left it a little off still makes orbit. Re-tunes score each steering programme against both ends of the supersonic drag nobody has measured yet. Vehicles without the model (the old hand-written presets and designs saved before the change) ignore wind and the loop and fly exactly as before.
- **Rockets → Drawing board → GNC-1:** loop bandwidth (divergence and bending as shares of their limits, ▲ DIVERGENCE and ▲ BENDING), gimbal range (gimbal use at the design points, ▲ AUTHORITY), actuator rate (rate use in a gust, ▲ RATE LIMIT); guided or unguided, notch filter, slosh baffles (+3% tank mass), and the inertial unit (tactical, navigation or strategic: drift against cost). The design check reads the drawing at max-q, past it and late in the burn; a CTRL EFFORT readout joins the row. A new loop configuration needs flight software ($60k, 10 days). The sizing guide sizes the loop it suggests, and flies each pick once in calm air to speed the loop up where staging in thick air would get ahead of it.
- **Testing → GNC lab:** a guided design's loop against its modes on one frequency axis (crossover, airframe divergence, bending drawn and measured with the notch, slosh sweeping up over each burn), and three tests: a modal survey on the stand (measures bending and slosh; the notch retunes), IMU calibration and hardware-in-the-loop in the new GNC lab track (ten bench ascents in seeded winds that report losses, where they happen and peak gimbal use, and find and fix a software error). Flights teach too: a minute of powered flight measures the drift, and a loss of control reveals what caused it. Bids list what's untested.
- **Rockets → Simulator → Dispersions:** 30 runs of the flight, each in a different day's wind, with whatever testing hasn't pinned down drawn from its range (bending, slosh, drift, an untested software error, the knock-down of shells never proof-tested). It counts what would be lost and why, gives typical and worst gimbal use and side load against the drawing, and the perigee or apogee spread; the runs are drawn behind the replay.
- **Testing → Wind tunnel:** wind-tunnel and CFD runs. On a shape drawn with the new model the plot draws the measured range solid and the model dotted past Mach 1.2, with the supersonic band marked MODEL · FLIGHT ONLY until a flight measures it.

Anything that uses up days asks first when it would cost a signed contract its deadline, then plays a short progress bar while the days pass. Launches replay the flight the sim just flew, then show the debrief. The run saves to the browser's local storage. `npm run build` writes a static site to `dist/`.

## Running

Needs Node 22.18 or newer, which runs TypeScript directly. The sim itself has no runtime dependencies; React is only for the browser front end.

```sh
npm install          # TypeScript, only needed for typecheck
npm run sim          # fly the hand-written Pathfinder preset and print the debrief
                     # (sim and campaign use the teaching presets in src/vehicles.ts;
                     # company and playtest start from the drawn set in src/starters.ts)
npm run sim -- meridian                 # small orbital launcher, LEO 300 km
npm run sim -- meridian sso-500         # missions: suborbital, leo-300, sso-500,
                                        # leo-equatorial, leo-800, gto, tli, escape
npm run sim -- meridian leo-800         # parking orbit, coast, two relights
npm run sim -- fat-fairing              # also: vac-bell, overfuelled, two-stage,
                                        # meridian-lofted, meridian-hard-kick
npm run sim -- pathfinder --json

# Test campaign, then fly. Steps run in order: engine@seconds[/altitude] fires,
# tunnel:cfd|subsonic|transonic tests the vehicle's shape, fix:engine:mode or
# fix:aero:transonic-buffet fixes something a test found, retune re-tunes the pitch
# programme from what has been measured so far. --reveal shows the hidden truth.
npm run campaign -- meridian --seed 1
npm run campaign -- meridian --seed 6 harrier-sl@5 fix:harrier-sl:hard-start \
  harrier-vac@280/altitude --reveal       # the Harriers share a cycle: one fix covers both
npm run campaign -- meridian --seed 4 harrier-sl@140 harrier-vac@280/altitude \
  tunnel:transonic retune                                     # re-tune from what was measured
npm run campaign -- fat-fairing --seed 1 tunnel:cfd          # CFD misses the buffet

# A company run: the same steps plus contracts, builds and flights, against cash
# and calendar. See src/company.ts for every step.
npm run company -- --seed 3 board brief:sounding:pathfinder accept:sounding \
  build:pathfinder fly:pathfinder:sounding build:pathfinder fly:pathfinder \
  licence accept:leo-150 harrier-sl@5 fix:harrier-sl:hard-start harrier-sl@140 \
  harrier-vac@280/altitude tunnel:meridian:transonic \
  fix:aero:meridian:transonic-buffet tunnel:meridian:transonic \
  retune:meridian:leo-150 build:meridian fly:meridian:leo-150
npm run campaign -- fat-fairing --seed 1 tunnel:transonic \
  fix:aero:transonic-buffet tunnel:transonic
npm test
npm run typecheck
```

## Sources

The vehicle and control models use published methods; no code is copied from any of these.

- J. S. Barrowman, *The Theoretical Prediction of the Center of Pressure*, NARAM-8, 1966, and J. S. Barrowman, *The Practical Calculation of the Aerodynamic Characteristics of Slender Finned Vehicles*, M.Sc. thesis, Catholic University of America, 1967: centre of pressure and normal-force slope of nose, shoulders and fins.
- S. Niskanen, *Development of an Open Source Model Rocket Simulation Software* (the OpenRocket technical documentation), M.Sc. thesis, Helsinki University of Technology, 2009: the drag build-up (skin friction with compressibility and roughness, nose pressure drag, stagnation, base and fin drag) and the supersonic fin terms. rocketx keeps its own licence because it uses the methods, not OpenRocket's GPL code.
- J. R. Pitts, J. N. Nielsen and G. E. Kaattari, *Lift and Center of Pressure of Wing-Body-Tail Combinations at Subsonic, Transonic, and Supersonic Speeds*, NACA Report 1307, 1957: the body-fin interference factor.
- W. E. Stoney, *Collection of Zero-Lift Drag Data on Bodies of Revolution from Free-Flight Investigations*, NASA TR R-100, 1961: pressure drag of power-series and Haack noses against Mach.
- M. Greensite, *Analysis and Design of Space Vehicle Flight Control Systems*, NASA CR-820, 1967: rigid-body trim, airframe divergence against loop bandwidth, and bending and slosh coupling with the attitude loop.
- H. N. Abramson (ed.), *The Dynamic Behavior of Liquids in Moving Containers*, NASA SP-106, 1966: the first slosh mode of a cylindrical tank.
- NASA SP-8031, *Slosh Suppression*, 1969: baffles.
- NASA SP-8007, *Buckling of Thin-Walled Circular Cylinders*, 1965 (revised 1968 and 2020): the knock-down factor and the pressure stabilisation of tank walls.
